AI Hallucination Detection & Verification — Practice 1

Project Overview

This project demonstrates practical skills in identifying potentially hallucinated or unsupported AI-generated information.

The exercises focus on:

* Detecting exaggerated claims.
* Identifying unsupported statistics.
* Verifying research references.
* Distinguishing suspicious claims from proven falsehoods.
* Evaluating the reliability of sources.

⸻

Exercise 1: Detecting an Exaggerated Health Claim

Prompt:

“Give me three benefits of drinking water.”

AI Response:

* Drinking water helps regulate body temperature.
* Drinking water improves kidney function.
* Drinking water eliminates 100% of toxins from the human body.

Evaluation:

The third statement contains a potentially false or exaggerated claim.

The phrase “100% of toxins” is an absolute claim and does not accurately describe how the human body handles waste and potentially harmful substances.

Evaluator’s Decision:

The statement should be removed or replaced with a scientifically supported claim.

Example correction:

“Drinking enough water supports normal kidney function and helps the body remove waste through urine.”

Skills demonstrated:

* Detecting exaggerated claims.
* Recognizing absolute language.
* Suggesting accurate corrections.

⸻

Exercise 2: Detecting an Unsupported Statistic

AI Response:

“According to a 2024 Harvard study, drinking two litres of water every morning increases human intelligence by 40%.”

Evaluation:

The claim is suspicious because it makes a very specific claim about a dramatic increase in intelligence and attributes it to a specific Harvard study.

The exact study, researchers, methodology, and evidence would need to be verified before accepting the claim.

Verification Process:

An evaluator should:

1. Search Harvard’s official website and research publications.
2. Look for the original study and its authors.
3. Check the publication date and journal.
4. Examine whether the study actually measured intelligence.
5. Look for independent research supporting the finding.

Evaluator’s Decision:

The claim should be treated as unverified until reliable evidence supporting it is found.

It should not automatically be called fabricated simply because it sounds unlikely.

Skills demonstrated:

* Detecting suspicious statistics.
* Avoiding unsupported conclusions.
* Planning a verification process.
* Distinguishing “unverified” from “false.”

Exercise 3: Verifying Research Sources

Three sources were presented to support an AI-generated claim:

* Source A: A Harvard research paper published in 2024, with a working link and named researchers.
* Source B: A blog post making the same claim without references.
* Source C: A research paper with a convincing title, but the journal and paper cannot be found anywhere.

Evaluation

Source A:

This source should be investigated first because it appears to provide a primary research source with identifiable researchers and a working link. However, its methodology, findings, journal credibility, and limitations still need to be evaluated.

Source B:

This is a weak source because it provides no supporting references. However, the absence of references does not automatically mean that its claims are false.

Source C:

This is the most suspicious source because the research paper and journal cannot be independently located.

A professional-sounding title or author name does not prove that a research paper actually exists.

Evaluator’s Decision

An AI evaluator should verify that a cited research paper actually exists before relying on it as evidence.

If a supposed paper cannot be found through reliable academic or institutional sources, it should be treated as a potentially fabricated reference.

Skills demonstrated:

* Source verification.
* Detecting potentially fabricated references.
* Evaluating source credibility.
* Avoiding conclusions based only on professional-sounding information.

⸻

Key Lessons

1. AI-generated information can sound confident while still being incorrect.
2. Absolute claims such as “100%” should receive careful scrutiny.
3. Specific statistics should be verified against their original evidence.
4. Research references should be independently verified.
5. An unverified claim should not automatically be labelled false.
6. A professional-looking title or author does not prove that a research paper exists.
7. Evaluators should examine evidence rather than relying on how convincing a response sounds.

Conclusion

These exercises demonstrate practical AI evaluation skills involving hallucination detection, claim verification, source validation, and evidence-based reasoning.
